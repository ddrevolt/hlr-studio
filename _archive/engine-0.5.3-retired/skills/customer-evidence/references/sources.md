# Where customer material comes from, and what each source does to it

Every source has a shape. The shape shows up in the findings unless it is named first. For each
source used, record what it over-samples and what it cannot contain.

## The general rule

**No source is a random sample of customers.** Review platforms are J-shaped — extreme-positive and
extreme-negative over-represented, the satisfied-and-silent majority absent. Support tickets sample
the dissatisfied who bothered to complain. Ad comments sample the reactive. Forums sample people
motivated enough to post, usually because something changed or went wrong. Interview panels sample
people willing to be interviewed. Say which of these you are working with, in the deliverable.

## Source shapes

| Source | Over-samples | Cannot contain | Native form to watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Public forums / communities | Active problem-solvers; transitions and crises; the engaged optimiser | Steady states — a person for whom nothing has changed has nothing to post | **Questions.** Forums are for asking; do not read help-seeking as evidence of how people behave off-forum |
| Product review pages | Extremes; recent purchasers; attribute-level comment | The non-purchaser; the person who returned it silently | Reviews are organised around product attributes, so texture/price/quantity talk is the *form*, not a finding |
| Support tickets | Problems, confusion, unmet expectations | Value received; the smooth majority | Categorise before analysing — bugs, confusion, missing features and expectation mismatches are different signals |
| Ad comments | Reactive, oppositional, and joke responses | Anyone who scrolled past | Objections here are the highest-value input for messaging, and the least representative of customers |
| Interview transcripts | The articulate and the available | Whoever declined | Retrospective accounts of decisions are reconstructions; treat causal claims as hypotheses |
| Survey open-ends | Whoever finished the survey | Abandoners | Open-ends and multiple-choice on the same topic often disagree; report both |
| Press coverage | The vivid | The typical | Journalist-selected for quotability. A press quote is not a customer verbatim |
| Brand-published testimonials | What marketing chose | Everything marketing didn't choose | **Not customer evidence at any strength.** Evidence about positioning |

## Choosing sources for a question

Match the source to what you need, and use more than one form so venue effects are visible:

- **Language and vocabulary** → reviews, forums, comments. Verbatim over paraphrase, always.
- **Purchase objections** → ad comments, sales calls, review one-and-two-stars, "vs" search content.
- **Trigger events** → forums (the "why now" posts), interviews, support tickets at onboarding.
- **Unmet needs** → "I wish," "why is there no," and workaround descriptions.
- **Competitive alternatives** → competitor reviews at three and four stars, switching stories.

## Recency

Weight the last twelve months more heavily and flag anything older. Markets move, products change,
and a three-year-old transcript may describe a different company. Where an older item is retained
because it is canonical, say so.

## The cold-start ladder

With no first-party material, walk outward and tag every output as provisional:

1. Your own differentiator — write it down as a hypothesis, not a finding.
2. Direct competitors' reviews — their customers describe the problem space in their words.
3. Comparable products in adjacent categories — same job, different solution.
4. Adjacent brands sharing the audience — what else this buyer buys.

Replace proxy evidence with first-party evidence as it arrives, and re-run rather than patch.

## Minimums

Five independent data points per segment before any persona or messaging conclusion. Two sources is
a lead worth targeted sampling, not a finding — and it should be reported in those words rather than
padded with adjacent material.
