# The refutation stage — full instructions

Hand these to analysts who have **not** seen how the claims were built.

## Packet for the refuters

Give them: the corpus (numbered items, each with source and date), and the claims stated bare —
label, central organising concept, item numbers, one quote. Where two analyses disagreed on
membership, show the disagreement and mark it as a target.

Withhold: the analytic decisions log, the disconfirming search already performed, the claimed
source counts, and any narrative about why a theme was formed. A refuter who knows the reasoning
argues with the reasoning instead of testing the claim.

Standing instruction to every lens: **assume the claim is wrong until the corpus forces you to
concede it. Where uncertain, side with "not supported."** Cite item numbers — a concern with no
item attached is not a finding.

---

## Lens 1 — Evidence audit

Check whether cited evidence bears the weight placed on it.

1. **Does each cited item say what the claim needs it to say?** Open every one. Quote the item when
   challenging its inclusion.
2. **Independent sources, not items.** Report the true count per claim; list which items collapse
   together; flag any claim reducing to one or two sources.
3. **Attribution integrity.** Flag every item that is not a customer speaking about their own
   experience: brand-published copy, a question posed to strangers, advice issued to others, a
   press-mediated quote, a third-party report, a quote about someone else. These cannot carry a
   claim about what customers experience.
4. **Prevalence overclaim.** Claims are worded as general statements. State the smallest wording
   the evidence actually supports.
5. **Shared evidence.** Which items work in multiple claims? Two claims resting on largely the same
   items are not independent findings — one may be derivative of the other.

Output per claim: **SUPPORTED / SUPPORTED-IF-NARROWED / NOT SUPPORTED**, true source count, specific
problems with item numbers, and for the middle verdict the exact narrower wording you would accept.

## Lens 2 — Alternative explanation

A claim earns its place only by beating the alternatives.

1. **The artifact test.** Could this pattern be produced by *where the data came from*? Forums
   reward asking; review pages reward complaint and are organised around product attributes; press
   quotes are selected by a journalist for vividness. If a claim's evidence concentrates in one
   venue form, the finding may be a property of the venue.

   Where you raise this, **name evidence that would escape the venue** — and check that it actually
   escapes. More material of the same kind, filtered for different content, does not: "posts where
   people describe what they did next" is still the forum. What escapes is a different mechanism —
   behavioural traces (purchase, return, support, search or usage logs), a prompted method where the
   researcher rather than the venue sets the agenda (interviews, diary studies), or a second venue
   whose incentives run the opposite way. A refutation that demands better evidence without naming
   what better looks like is an objection, not a finding.
2. **The mundane alternative.** Is there an ordinary account that does not need the claim's
   construct? Would this also be true of anyone dieting, ill, busy, grieving, living alone, or
   short of money? A claim that is not specific to this group is much weaker than it looks.
3. **Construct inflation.** Does the claim name a felt state or belief where the items only show a
   behaviour?
4. **Direction of the arrow.** Where a claim implies cause, could it run the other way, or could
   both sides share a third cause?
5. **Is it one claim or two?** Where a claim joins ideas with "and" or "so," test whether the join
   is earned or whether the halves rest on different items — and whether they contradict.

Output per claim: **HOLDS / HOLDS-WEAKLY / BETTER EXPLAINED OTHERWISE**, the strongest alternative
you can construct, which items support it, and what evidence would decide between them.

## Lens 3 — Disconfirming hunt

Find what contradicts. Do not summarise the claims or restate their support.

1. **Read all items against each claim**, not only the cited ones. The most valuable counter-example
   is usually one the claim never mentioned.
2. **Label what you find:**
   - *Contradicting* — says the opposite.
   - *Bounding* — the claim holds only under a condition, so the general wording is too broad.
   - *Internally complicating* — an item the claim **cites** that, read fully, undercuts it. Most
     damaging, easiest to miss, because it was counted as support.
3. **When nothing is found, say what that means.** Is the claim robust, or could this corpus not
   contain a counter-example? Name the item that *would* have counted. A corpus of event-driven
   posts cannot contain "nothing has changed and this is just how I eat now," so a claim about
   instability is unfalsifiable there — and that is a finding about the claim, not support for it.
4. **The silent majority.** Who would have to be in the corpus to contradict this, and do they post
   at all?

Then, separately: **name any pattern in the corpus that no claim captures.** Uncited items are where
to look. What people are saying that the claim set misses is often more valuable than the claims.

Output per claim: disconfirming items with number, exact quote, and type; one line on survival. Then
the missed-pattern section. Then which claim is most damaged and which is genuinely robust.

---

## Adjudication

Survives only where **every** lens clears it. Any lens returning "not supported" or "better explained
otherwise" fails the claim as worded. Where a lens offers narrower wording, the narrower wording is
what survives.

**Do not average verdicts.** One well-evidenced refutation beats two passes that found nothing —
finding nothing is weak evidence, finding something specific is strong evidence.

Record failed claims with their reason rather than deleting them. A withdrawn claim with its
refutation on the record is more useful than a clean list, and it stops the same claim being
rediscovered next quarter.
